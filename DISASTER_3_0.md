## Disaster 3.0: live information, human agency

For fifteen years, the central insight has been clear: people closest to an event hold information no central system can reliably gather on its own. A witness sees the fire on their block. A neighbor knows who cannot evacuate without help. A survivor can describe their own condition. Each person can contribute value—if the system gives them a trusted way to do so.

That need is now recognized across emergency response, recovery, and governance. Live, ground-level information can change what happens next. Yet people still lack a shared mechanism for contributing it with consent, provenance, and control over what they disclose and to whom.

**Disaster 3.0 is the shift from mapping a disaster to enabling people to coordinate through it.** County GIS shows the hazard, affected places, and damaged assets. Has-Needs adds what people see, need, offer, and accomplish there. Reports remain connected to their source; independent observations can corroborate one another; offers become visible commitments; and outcomes become part of the record.

This changes the operating model at every level. People become active sources of situational awareness and participants in response. Local knowledge can move through an edge-enabled network without waiting for every report to travel up and back down a hierarchy. Agencies gain a live view of Needs, resources, assignments, bottlenecks, and completed work. Communities gain agency, accountability, and a durable record of what they contributed and received.

The change also reframes data value. In a fast-moving emergency, information is valuable because it is fresh enough to guide action. Has-Needs makes that information useful without requiring people to surrender control of their personal data. Consent governs disclosure; provenance preserves source; reciprocal receipts document accepted exchanges and outcomes.

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires make the difference concrete. The after-action record documents rapidly changing fire conditions, ground reports, communication vulnerabilities, and long intervals between observations, command awareness, and action. A mapped perimeter shows where the fire went. A Disaster 3.0 record can also show what people saw as it moved, when they reported it, who received the report, how independent reports converged, what resources were committed, and what happened next. ([LA County after-action review collection](https://lacounty.gov/aar/); [McChrystal alert and evacuation review](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/bos/supdocs/207915.pdf); [Citygate Eaton Fire investigation](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/lac/1208708_InvestigationReportoftheEatonFireEvacuationAlerts_05-18-26_.pdf))

That is the quantum jump: from a picture of the event to a living, person-centered coordination system; from people represented as data points to people able to contribute value on their own terms; from reconstructing the response afterward to seeing its needs, commitments, bottlenecks, and outcomes as they unfold.

Has-Needs is the mechanism for that transition. It lets everyone contribute while preserving consent, provenance, and personal control—and makes the resulting information actionable across the network.
