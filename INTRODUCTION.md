# Has-Needs

*What do you have? What do you need? What can we accomplish together?*

In a disaster, a person under the rubble knows their own condition and immediate surroundings. If they can communicate, they may know nearby dangers, who else needs help, what they have to contribute, and whether rescuers should focus on the building next door. No amount of technology and money can remotely supply that person’s firsthand understanding.

People closest to danger hold information that institutions cannot gather from a distance. Yet they often lack authority over whether their knowledge is heard, how it is recorded, and what others do with it. A report can become institutional data while the person who supplied it has little control over the record or the decisions it informs.

Owning your data changes that relationship. Has-Needs is designed as the first comprehensive societal tool set to make each person’s authority over their identity, knowledge, needs, contributions, and participation practical and enforceable. People choose what to disclose, to whom, and under what terms; retain their own records; and carry evidence of agreements and outcomes into future interactions. Shared by choice, those records can contribute to collective memory. Has-Needs begins from the premise that each person is an expert on their own condition, circumstances, knowledge, and priorities.

Has-Needs is built around four elements:

- **Sovereign individual:** the person whose authority over their participation Has-Needs is designed to make enforceable; they decide what they offer, what they seek, and how they participate.
- **Has:** something available to contribute: knowledge, skills, resources, time, or an opportunity.
- **Need:** something to obtain, accomplish, learn, repair, or change.
- **Community:** people gathered around a shared purpose. A community can express its own Has and Needs while each member retains authority over their participation.

Has-Needs lets people coordinate locally, whether forming a group for the first time or rebuilding from community remnants. By the time aid arrives, they can provide a coherent picture of what is happening, what is needed, and what they can contribute. Contribution is never a condition for rescue or aid. People can also organize support before a crisis and sustain care and healing afterward.

This is a post-capitalist, non-extractive economic design that is particularly well suited to disaster. It also supports everyday exchange—care, knowledge, barter, mutual aid, or payment, as participants decide—and can provide verifiable creative provenance and support meaningful participation in governance, including for people and communities at the edges of conventional value chains.

## How cooperation works

People describe Has and Need in their own terms and choose the context they provide. The objects circulate independently to reveal possible matches. AI can help interpret language and suggest candidates; people decide what fits and whether to proceed. Mutual consent moves a pairing into **WORKING**, where participants develop and carry out their agreement. A Need-owned journal records the developing work. At final resolution, participants mutually attest to the outcome in identical receipts written simultaneously to each participant’s personal chain.

Each person retains their own history. Reciprocal receipts connect independent histories into a **forest of chains**. Together, these records can preserve claims and how understanding changes. Has-Needs is a lightweight mechanism that lets recorded behavior, as participants choose to disclose it, dictate the impressions others form. It assigns no universal trust score. Participants can add support, context, or evidence for a more accurate account. The collective value is shared memory and its capacity for correction—not a price on what is true or a central verdict about it. Has-Needs is not a system for describing resources. It is a system for discovering and preserving successful state transitions. The ontology is not the truth; it is the current map of what has worked. Completed exchanges and participant input can inform future discovery, while local meanings remain in view and communities can compare understandings without requiring one central vocabulary.

The same relationships compose across individuals, communities, and institutions. People may form a community through the work they choose to do together. A local group can coordinate with another group or an institution while its members retain their own authority. This is designed emergence: wider organization grows from participants’ actions and agreements.

Participation begins locally and can extend through available peer-to-peer links and accessible transports, including text, speech, SMS, terminal, and assistive interfaces. The envisioned Globe UI presents possibilities in relation to a person’s surroundings while keeping precise location under that person’s control. A Persona Manager lets people engage through different facets while controlling what each one discloses.

Has-Needs emerged from a lifetime of careful observation across very different domains: personal GIS, fire, trauma and emergency response, community self-determination, sailing, and crisis mapping. These realms converged in a perfect storm during the 2010 Haiti response, when I was told that people in disaster could not be trusted to report their own conditions. I knew otherwise, especially in disaster extremis, and committed to building “trustability” for firsthand reports—come hell or high water—without taking authority from the people who made them. From 2010 to 2015, my full-time SBTF volunteering across the full range of mass events became a crucible in which I tested and rapidly refined the design. Community mapping and Indigenous approaches to sovereignty further shaped its respect for who holds knowledge and under what conditions it may be shared. Read more in [History](./HISTORY.md).
