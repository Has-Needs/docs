# Has-Needs

**What do you have? What do you need? What can we accomplish together?**

## The person closest to the event

In a disaster, the person trapped under rubble is the only firsthand expert on their own condition and immediate surroundings. They can say, “I’m OK for now. Check the preschool next door.” Bluetooth discovery opens a startling route through that isolation: a person inside the rubble can find and communicate with a passerby outside. Any bystander nearby can become a human link to the world beyond the debris—hearing what is needed and helping the person direct what happens next. Secure provenance travels with every message, keeping its origin and handoffs verifiable from the person in the rubble, through the bystander and responders, all the way up the decision chain. No amount of technology or money can remotely supply that firsthand understanding.

Has-Needs gives that knowledge a direct route into collective action. People express what they need and what they have; local peer-to-peer links connect requests with nearby offers. In a disaster, that can mean finding a safe place to sleep or offering a spare room. People already organize this kind of help; Has-Needs is designed to make offers and requests feel safer to share, invite more people to contribute, and help extend the benevolence that often follows a disaster. By showing needs and capacities together, it also gives people a fuller perspective on one another and makes empathy easier to act on. Independent reports corroborate and correct the shared picture.

That rescue is a reciprocal exchange. The survivor holds location and condition no outside system can buy or supply; rescuers hold the ability to reach them. The survivor can choose to share only those facts for rescue. Each offers something priceless to the other.

I chose to record value exchanges because the foundation should recognize people through what they agree to do for one another. A Need gives a person’s stated need standing; a Has makes another person’s capacity to help visible. Reciprocity creates the expectation that a contribution will be answered. That recognition helps people feel valued by the system and strengthens agency. The receipt proves each person’s part in the agreement and records the outcome as a fact. Durable provenance keeps the exchange connected to its source and status, so it is not lost in the shuffle or confused with a repeat message. This is designed emergence: reciprocal records make recognition, agency, and accountable cooperation part of the system’s ordinary operation.

The Los Angeles County after-action reviews document communications vulnerabilities and inconsistent field reporting during the Eaton and Palisades fires. [Read the County reviews](https://lacounty.gov/aar/), and see [our analysis of those fire records against the current Has-Needs workflow](./LA_FIRE_RECORDS_HAS_NEEDS_ANALYSIS.md).

The same simple pattern—what people have, what they need, and what they agree to do together—cuts across all human activity, from disaster response to agriculture, care, learning, research, and commerce. Working with the Persona Manager, each person contributes through chosen facets, with control over what each facet discloses and which actions it authorizes. Reciprocal records connect those permissions and contributions to the agreements and outcomes they produce. Together, these elements enable unprecedented security and trustability for individual contributions and collective outputs.

## A simple structure for cooperation

Has-Needs is grounded in two tenets: sovereignty and local-first operation. The sovereign person is primary; cooperation starts with that person’s choices and grows outward through local connections. People can work from their own devices and synchronize with peers as connections allow. The system has four elements:

- **A sovereign person** who chooses what to offer, request, accept, and disclose.
- **Has**: something available to contribute, such as a skill, resource, time, knowledge, or opportunity.
- **Need**: a requested outcome expressed as a contract, with terms for working toward it.
- **Community**: people gathered around a shared purpose.

Has and Need circulate independently so people can discover possible matches. A match is an invitation, not a commitment. The people involved decide whether it fits. With mutual consent, the Has and Need enter the **WORKING** state under their chosen terms. The work ends in fulfillment, partial fulfillment, or a decision not to proceed. Participants preserve reciprocal evidence of the outcome on their own chains.

The same relationships support a neighbor helping a neighbor, a group coordinating a delivery, or institutions working with communities. Groups form through the agreements people choose. This is designed emergence: coordination grows from the same simple elements at every scale, while each person retains authority over their own participation.

## Sovereignty through participation

Each person has a private core and can use different facets for different relationships. This separates contexts while preserving that person’s authority across collective work.

AI helps interpret different words and surface possible matches. People decide what fits and whether to participate. Shared understanding grows from local language, context, and exchanges that work. Communities can compare and revise their understandings without adopting one central vocabulary.

The Globe UI presents a personal geographic view of relevant possibilities. JPEG 2000 supports my georeferenced raster approach; rendered in RGB layers, it becomes a visual “lingua franca” for analysis by people without specialist GIS training. People can compare and combine layers to see spatial relationships directly, without translating local knowledge into institutional categories first. Text, speech, SMS, terminal, and assistive interfaces extend participation through different modalities.

## Why it exists

Has-Needs grew from a lifetime drive to make globe-based mapping a human interaction system and a way for people to see and use data. It began with environmental education and play, then developed through georeferenced mapping, crisis response, and computer networks I designed for nonprofit organizations. I wanted a globe where information could be seen in context and people could act on it.

My design choices also come from work where timing, trust, and accountability carry human consequences: military logistics and mobile operations; paramedicine, all-environment rescue, and mass-casualty response; social-justice organizing; and work with communities asserting sovereignty. Trauma recovery taught me that agency and personal accountability are central to recovery. People need control over what they share and a way to make their commitments visible and answerable.

Haiti was the collision point. Late one night at a Haiti hackathon, I watched some of the best minds in disaster response pulling their hair out, searching for datasets that could tell them more about conditions on the ground. I suggested letting people call in their preferences and needs. The suggestion was laughed at. That moment showed me how citizen input was regarded, even when responders urgently needed firsthand information. I also saw a new use for Bluetooth discovery: the rubble need not cut a person off from the human world. A passerby could become reachable from inside; the person trapped below could be heard, ask for help, and enlist someone already walking past. I vowed then to make people’s contributions trustable, while keeping people in authority over what they share and what they agree to do. Has-Needs grew from that vow: people express Has and Need, choose terms together, and preserve reciprocal records of what they agreed and what happened.

Has-Needs is buildable now. I have prototypes, and its functional components are complete: Need contracts use eUTXO; Has is represented by NFTs; DXOS supports local-first collaboration; and OCA provides layered data context. Jitterbug supplies an adaptive peer-to-peer network topology for discovery and routing, a plausibly novel design. Has-Needs brings these components together through sovereign agreements and reciprocal records.

The structure applies to ordinary life as readily as disaster: sharing a harvest, arranging a repair, teaching a skill, or coordinating a delivery. Everyday use builds useful relationships and evidence before a crisis. **Tell the story from disaster to everyday life; test from everyday life toward disaster.**
