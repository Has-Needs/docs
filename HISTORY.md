# Has-Needs — origins and development history

A personal account of the experiences, ideas, and work behind Has-Needs.

## Dedication to Ishi

> This work is dedicated to the memory and legacy of Ishi, whose story as the last known member of his people echoed through my own foster-childhood—teaching me resilience, and perspective. In Ishi’s quiet courage and unbroken dignity, I learned the value of personal sovereignty and how mutual respect knits impossible divides.

## Childhood, movement, and continuity

I entered foster care in seventh grade. In an advanced English class, a teacher brought river stones from the region associated with Ishi and invited us to choose one while reading his story. Nature was already a source of refuge, hope, and strength for me, and I felt an affinity with the displacement and isolation in that story. I later lost the stone, like many treasured belongings during childhood moves.

I moved more than fifty times before age twenty. Those years included stays in a castle in Italy, a cottage in Grasse, and a château in Antibes, followed by hardship in California, food scavenging, and foster care. Each relocation brought a different world of relationships, dangers, opportunities, and local knowledge. Decisions might have been different if more of the surrounding situation had been visible to me. Gaps in memory associated with trauma made preserving personally meaningful history especially important.

These experiences shaped my concern for the **self-quantified sovereign individual**: retaining continuity of identity, relationships, contributions, and commitments whether rooted or mobile.

## Agency, trauma, and systems thinking

My early exposure to Buckminster Fuller, peer counseling since 1985, emergency and mass-casualty response as a paramedic, post-trauma debriefing, and participation in a UCSF stress/trauma study informed my thinking about agency and resilience.

The continuing design goal became trauma mitigation through practical empowerment: give people direct ways to speak, choose, contribute, and cooperate. **Sovereign Survivor +1** expresses this starting point—a survivor as a sovereign actor, able to connect with at least one other sovereign participant. People affected by a disaster have knowledge of their circumstances and need timely information themselves.

## Early activism and community sovereignty

Environmental and social-justice organizing preceded the software. I had been involved in activism since childhood. My first independent campaign was at **UCSB**, concerning university investment in apartheid-era South Africa and support for **Nelson Mandela**. I continued participating in social movements, including the **WTO protests in Seattle** and protests in **Melbourne, Australia**.

Around **2000**, I worked directly with a family group among the **Dja Dja Wurrung (Jaara) Traditional Owners** of the Bendigo region in Victoria, Australia. A different Indigenous organization, without ancestral affiliation to the local clan, was administering government funding through the area's community centre. I undertook forensic computer analysis of the administrative records and identified what I considered financial misuse benefiting associates of the administrators. My findings helped the Dja Dja Wurrung family group regain control of the community centre.

I remember that return of local control enabling culturally appropriate administration and community services, with work toward repatriation of cultural artifacts, cultural education, and health-related facilities on their Country. It was a practical experience of community sovereignty: people gaining control of the resources intended for them.

The **2013 Dja Dja Wurrung Recognition and Settlement Agreement** with the State of Victoria was a later milestone in formal recognition of Traditional Owner rights.

## Global networks and local relevance

Early membership in Nomads International, international bulletin board systems, and 2600-oriented network culture brought excitement at the collapse of distance. Commercial platforms subsequently enclosed and partitioned social groups.

My attention turned toward seeing the world immediately around oneself differently. In a disaster, when possessions and institutions may disappear, a person still occupies physical space. I began asking: *Who does my location matter to? When? Where do interests intersect, and how can those intersections be supported?*

## 1996–97: Environmental engagement and the satellite globe idea

The starting idea was a **land-sailing app that made environmental education feel like play**. In 1996–97, I was developing a handheld wind sail and envisioned pairing sailing with a globe showing environmental conditions. Wind, geography, and air quality directly affected where people could go and how much fun they would have. Airborne particulates could spoil an outing, making environmental conditions personally relevant.

The globe evolved toward general-purpose geographic visualization and the mapping of human needs. **EnterChange** was another incarnation of that broader vision.

I recall speaking in 1996 or 1997 with aeronautics researcher **Ilan Kroo**, who had worked at NASA Ames, about using satellite imagery draped over an interactive globe.

## November 1999: NASA technology exhibition in Miami Beach

I recall discussing the globe and satellite-data access with NASA representatives at a technology exposition at a large white resort in Miami Beach. I was told that work on a similar globe was already under way. This surprised me because I had previously raised the idea and had not received subsequent communication about its development.

**Tech East ’99**, held **November 1–3, 1999** at the **Fontainebleau Hilton**, is a possible identification of the event.

## 2002–07: World Wind and the developer community

NASA World Wind development began in 2002, with the original application publicly released in 2004. The open-source application and its community enabled developers to layer and explore geographic information on a virtual globe.

I recall an active World Wind community in approximately **2005–07**, including discussions with **UN and NASA representatives in the community forum**. Open-source tools were strongly favored in those discussions, while access to consistent, useful geospatial data remained a challenge.

**In 2006–07, I developed a NASA World Wind plugin** that ingested CSV files and presented georeferenced information on the globe using icons and colors. The intent was to make disparate data sources useful for human-oriented geographic visualization, including expressing people's needs.

I also followed GIS-based disaster reporting and worked with **Greg Gollberg in Idaho** on a GIS fire-prediction environment.

## 2007–09: Google Earth, the UN, and the Millennium Development Goals

In **November 2007**, the **United Nations Development Programme, Google, and Cisco** introduced the **Millennium Development Goals Monitor**, including a Google Earth layer for geographic development indicators.

This surprised me after the World Wind community discussions. We had hoped that World Wind's open-source tools would be used for efforts of this kind. Access to useful, reliable data remained a broader challenge regardless of the visualization platform.

Both the **2008 World Business and Development Awards** and the **2009 Imagine Cup** were relevant to this period. I submitted **the finished visualization project, its NASA World Wind CSV-to-globe plugin, and a working demonstration to the Imagine Cup**, whose 2009 competition theme addressed the Millennium Development Goals.

## 2009: Emergency-management coursework

In **2009**, I was taking **emergency-management courses at Capella University**, continuing my study of disaster response, coordination, and community resilience before the Haiti earthquake.

## 2010: The Haiti turning point

During the Haiti earthquake response hackathons, I watched humanitarian, academic, and technical specialists struggle to obtain timely information about conditions on the ground. I suggested that affected people could call and report their observations and needs. I recall the room laughing and being told that human reports were regarded as untrustworthy across the response system.

A call-in mechanism subsequently received citizen messages, translated and categorized them, and fed them into the Needs reporting system. My broader proposal for sovereign, reciprocal participation was not pursued there.

The distinction between **collecting information from a person** and **empowering that person to participate** became central. Securing the individual and enabling direct discovery, cooperation, agreements, and reciprocal history became the motivating architecture of Has-Needs.

## 2010–13: UMass Boston

After Haiti, I attended a **post-earthquake forum at the University of Massachusetts Boston**. The people I met and the institution's approach convinced me that targeted academic study could help advance Has-Needs. I pursued **dual degrees in psychology and sociology**, graduating in **2013**.

I deliberately shaped my curriculum around **trauma, Indigenous studies, and epigenetics** to inform the architecture. I wanted to understand how people recover agency, exercise sovereignty, participate in communities, and respond to adversity.

Has-Needs has been a dedicated design effort since the 2010 Haiti response, building on the earlier environmental, geographic, and social-justice work.

## Participatory mapping and community knowledge

Map Kibera and WaveFax were formative observations. My experience also included participatory action research, experience sampling, citizen science, SBTF crisis mapping, Uchaguzi, UN-HHI exercises, and photomapping work at Amazon and IBM.

These experiences brought attention to local knowledge that was otherwise difficult to see or use, and to the dependence of many information systems on institutions and intermediaries.

The **Globe UI** developed as a medium of exchange and relevant interaction across languages, cultures, and literacy levels. Pictorial representations of surroundings could connect information with a person's understanding of place. Speech, text, SMS, screen readers, and other accessible pathways remained part of the design.

## 2010s–early 2020s: Cryptography, Web3, and interchain evaluation

I spent years following advances in cryptography, blockchain systems, **Web3**, and technologies for connecting or coordinating multiple chains. I evaluated them against the requirements of individual ownership and continuity, contextual disclosure, voluntary agreements, reciprocal evidence, local-first use, and cooperation under personal authority.

None of the external chain or interchain architectures I examined met the requirements as a whole. Independently identifiable objects, condition-governed transactions, append-only provenance, and verifiable agreement informed the design. Has-Needs uses eUTXO and NFT concepts as architectural blueprints, with its own receipt histories and sovereign interaction model.

## 2022 onward: AI-assisted research and architectural development

Beginning in 2022, generative AI became a research and design instrument. It helped me examine blockchain and interchain technologies, understand security mechanisms and operational requirements, compare alternatives, and explore different ways to achieve the protections the architecture needed.

The process was iterative: exposing constraints, explaining workflows, testing ideas, identifying commonalities, and reducing unnecessary complexity.

Conversations with Perplexity helped crystallize the semantic triplet **[entity, relation, context]**. It brought different human interactions into a common grammar while allowing people to begin with their own dialect and local understanding. Ontology could grow outward through human contact, agreed meanings, and completed exchanges.

The continuing question was:

**What is the smallest set of mechanisms that preserves individual sovereignty, verifiable cooperation, and the ability to accommodate the full range of human interactions?**

My influences also included **Buckminster Fuller's systems thinking** and **DSRP**—Distinctions, Systems, Relationships, Perspectives—associated with Derek and Laura Cabrera. David Graeber and David Wengrow's *The Dawn of Everything* informed my reflections on social diversity, settlement, and movement.

## Knowledge exchange and personal continuity

The economic vision gives people and communities authority over the knowledge they produce and the terms of its use. A farmer's old phone observing field conditions was one example explored during the design: observations could be pooled locally and offered through voluntary agreements to agencies or researchers. Participation could be passive while authorization remained sovereign. Payment and markets could coexist with mutual aid and other forms of exchange.

Personal continuity also informed the exploration of owner-held provenance and consent for writings, creative work, voice, images, and other personal artifacts, including their use in AI-derived representations.
