# Has-Needs — origins and development history

This is a historical companion to [Has-Needs Specification V1](./Has-Needs-Spec-v1.md), not part of the protocol's normative requirements. It preserves firsthand recollections, project chronology, and relevant external records. Where a connection is based on the founder's recollection rather than an independently located record, the distinction is made in the narrative.

## Longstanding interests

The roots of Has-Needs include geographic information, environmental engagement, social systems, disaster resilience, and a deeply personal understanding of the consequences of having little voice over decisions that affect one's life.

The founder's work and reading across communities and cultures, childhood experiences within caregiving and foster-care systems, and emergency medical work sustained an interest in how people cooperate and how institutions behave when normal support structures fail. The continuing design goal is trauma mitigation through practical empowerment: give people direct ways to speak, choose, contribute, and cooperate.

## Early activism and social systems in practice

Environmental and social-justice organizing preceded the software. In the founder's account, he had been involved in activism since childhood. His first independent campaign was at **UCSB**, concerning university investment in apartheid-era South Africa and support for **Nelson Mandela**. He continued participating in social movements, including the **WTO protests in Seattle** and **Melbourne, Australia**.

His work extended beyond protests. He recalls spending approximately a year supporting an Indigenous group in Australia and contributing to an effort that culminated in what he describes as a **landmark 2013 sovereignty decision involving the state of Victoria**. The group's name and the decision's formal citation remain to be added.

These experiences supplied a practical understanding of how people organize, how institutions distribute authority, and what meaningful self-determination requires. They are part of the foundation of Has-Needs' community-sovereignty and trauma-mitigation design—not merely episodes preceding its technical development.

## 1996–97: Environmental engagement and the satellite globe idea

The starting idea was a **land-sailing app that made environmental education feel like play**. In 1996–97, the founder was developing a handheld wind sail and envisioned pairing the enjoyment of sailing with a globe showing environmental conditions. People would have a reason to notice wind, geography, and air quality because those conditions directly affected where they could go and how much fun they would have. When airborne particulates spoiled an outing or made a location undesirable, environmental awareness would arise from lived experience rather than an abstract lesson.

The playful interface was intentional: **invite people in through something enjoyable, then give them a direct personal reason to care about their environment.** The globe evolved from that idea toward general-purpose geographic visualization and the mapping of human needs. **EnterChange** was another incarnation of that broader vision.

He recalls speaking in 1996 or 1997 with aeronautics researcher **Ilan Kroo**, who had worked at NASA Ames, about using satellite imagery draped over an interactive globe. The founder regards that proposal as an inspiration for the eventual NASA World Wind project. The conversation and causal connection are presented as his firsthand account; a contemporaneous project record establishing influence has not yet been located.

## November 1999: NASA technology exhibition in Miami Beach

The founder recalls discussing his desired globe and satellite-data access with NASA representatives at a technology exposition held at a very large white resort in Miami Beach. The venue and period correspond to **Tech East ’99**, held **November 1–3, 1999** at the **Fontainebleau Hilton**. At this event he recalls being told that work on a similar globe was already under way. This was surprising because he had previously raised the idea and had not received subsequent communication about its development.

That conversation belongs to the chronology of his own work; it does not establish a documented NASA decision about how World Wind originated.

## 2002–07: World Wind and the developer community

NASA World Wind development began in 2002, with the original application publicly released in 2004. The open-source application and its community enabled developers to layer and explore geographic information on a virtual globe.

The founder recalls a particularly active World Wind community in approximately **2005–07**, including discussions with **UN and NASA representatives in the community forum**. Open-source tools were strongly favored within these discussions, and access to consistent, useful geospatial data was an ongoing challenge.

**In 2006–07, he developed a NASA World Wind plugin** that ingested CSV files and presented georeferenced information on the globe using icons and colors. The intent was to make disparate sources of data useful for human-oriented geographic visualization, including expressing people's needs.

Separately, he followed GIS-based disaster reporting and worked with **Greg Gollberg in Idaho** on a GIS fire-prediction environment; the precise dates of that collaboration remain to be determined.

## 2007–09: Google Earth, the UN, and the Millennium Development Goals

In **November 2007**, the **United Nations Development Programme, Google, and Cisco** publicly introduced the **Millennium Development Goals Monitor**, including a Google Earth layer for geographic development indicators.

From the founder's perspective, this was a surprise to the World Wind community. Its discussions included UN and NASA participants, and members understood open-source software to have strong institutional appeal. The developer community had hoped that World Wind's open-source geospatial tools would be used for efforts of this kind. The recollection concerns expectations and conversations within that community, **not a documented UN-wide rule that proprietary tools could never be used**.

The broader challenge was also about **access to data**, not only the visualization platform. A virtual globe could display many forms of information, but useful, reliable data remained difficult to acquire and integrate.

The founder identifies **both the 2008 World Business and Development Awards and the 2009 Imagine Cup** as events relevant to this period. He specifically confirms submitting **the finished visualization project, its NASA World Wind CSV-to-globe plugin, and a working demonstration to the Imagine Cup**, whose 2009 competition theme addressed the Millennium Development Goals. The exact submission date, competition category, and original entry materials still await archival confirmation.

Related historically documented initiatives include:
- The [UNDP/Google/Cisco Millennium Development Goals Monitor announcement (November 2007)](https://maps.googleblog.com/2007/11/introducing-mgd-monitor.html).
- The [UN Millennium Development Goals High-level Event (September 2008)](https://www.un.org/en/conferences/environment/newyork2008).
- The [2008 World Business and Development Awards call](https://iccwbo.org/news-publications/news/2008-world-business-and-development-awards-launched/), supported by UNDP and partners.
- The [2009 Imagine Cup](https://connect.unevoc.unesco.org/home/Microsoft%2BImagine%2BCup%2B2009), a technology competition with a Millennium Development Goals theme and UNESCO involvement.

**The Imagine Cup is now identified as the submission destination.** The other MDG-era events are retained here as historical context and part of the founder's recollection, not as additional claimed submission destinations.

## 2010: The Haiti turning point

The Haiti earthquake response hackathons brought geographic information, human experience, and cryptographic sovereignty into sharp alignment.

The founder recalls watching a remarkable concentration of humanitarian, academic, and technical expertise struggle to obtain timely information about conditions on the ground. He suggested that affected people themselves could call and report their observations and needs. He recalls the room laughing and being told that human reports were regarded as untrustworthy across the response system.

A call-in mechanism did subsequently emerge that received citizen messages, translated and categorized them, and fed them into the Needs reporting system. The founder regards this as a direct consequence of the discussion, while his broader proposal for sovereign, reciprocal participation was not pursued there.

The foundational realization was the distinction between **collecting information from a person** and **empowering that person to be an active participant**. Securing the individual and enabling direct discovery, cooperation, agreements, and reciprocal history became the motivating architecture of Has-Needs.
