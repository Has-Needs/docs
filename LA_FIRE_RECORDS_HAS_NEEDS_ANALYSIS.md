# Los Angeles Fire Records, Human Costs, and the Economics of Coordination

**A records-based operational replay of the January 2025 Eaton and Palisades fires through the current Has-Needs workflow**

## Purpose

This analysis places the Los Angeles County fire records beside the Has-Needs workflow and the financial scale of emergency response and recovery. It asks what a participant-controlled system could have represented, connected, and preserved: needs, offers, resource commitments, communications, and outcomes.

Has-Needs was not deployed during these fires. This is a retrospective comparison, not a claim that it changed the response or its outcomes. The County reviews and economic analyses show the human and financial conditions against which the design can be evaluated.

## The event: competing demands and limited visibility

The Eaton and Palisades fires killed 31 people and destroyed more than 16,000 structures. Nearly 250,000 people were placed under evacuation warnings or orders. The County’s alert review describes communications vulnerabilities, inconsistent field reporting, and uneven real-time access to information. It identifies unreliable cellular service, power shutoffs affecting cell towers, heavy smoke, and disconnected platforms among the conditions that disrupted information flow. Some residents reported that they received no warning before evacuating or received it hours after they had left. ([County after-action review collection](https://lacounty.gov/aar/); [McChrystal alert and evacuation review](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/bos/supdocs/207915.pdf))

The Eaton timeline shows scarcity in motion. From about **1:00 to 2:00 a.m. on January 8**, field resources were handling approximately **30 separate structure fires**—about one new fire-response request every two minutes—while triaging a limited number of available resources. At **2:18 a.m.**, command heard critical radio reports of fire moving west toward northwestern Altadena. The process to evaluate expanded evacuation orders and warnings west of Lake Avenue began. Pasadena police reported a street on fire west of Lake Avenue at **2:33 a.m.**; sheriff’s personnel reported fire on both sides of Lake Avenue and approaching homes at **2:43 a.m.** Orders for western Altadena zones went out by about **3:25 a.m.** ([Citygate investigation, revised May 19, 2026](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/lac/1208708_InvestigationReportoftheEatonFireEvacuationAlerts_05-18-26_.pdf))

Citygate concluded that Incident Command did not fail to request earlier orders west of Lake Avenue: the process began when the westward spread became known to command at approximately 2:18 a.m. Its timeline also records that command had no real-time aerial view of fire spread in the mountains, and that darkness, extreme winds, and scarce resources shaped the situation. This analysis examines how information and requests move through a response. It does not assign fire losses to a delay in the order process.

The County’s later review of recovery and repopulation covers the three months following the fires. Its six focus areas include communications; volunteer and donation management; recovery; repopulation; shelter, housing, and people at higher risk; and public health and access and functional needs. It reports **26 findings and 25 recommendations**. Those topics matter because emergencies continue after the evacuation: people need a place to sleep, food, medicine, transportation, accessible services, reliable information, and ways to offer help. ([County review overview and documents](https://lacounty.gov/aar/); [recovery review announcement](https://lacounty.gov/2026/09/11/la-county-releases-second-after-action-review-covering-recovery-and-repopulation-operations-following-2025-wildfires/))

## The costs people experience

In a large emergency, needs and resources collide at once. A family may be searching for a room while residents with spare rooms are trying to offer them. Responders may receive many reports while lacking the staff to investigate each one. A shelter may have capacity that is difficult to find or unsuitable for a person with access needs. Some people can reach official systems; others are cut off by power loss, weak cellular coverage, disability, language, displacement, or lack of an established institutional connection.

When coordination depends on scattered calls, forms, spreadsheets, and platforms, emergency workers spend time collecting, re-entering, checking, and reconciling information. A request can be repeated without its history; an offer can remain invisible; two groups can commit resources to one visible problem while another need goes unanswered. These are measurable costs in staff time, response time, duplicated effort, unfilled needs, and resources committed without a confirmed outcome. The LA reviews document the communications, volunteer, donation, shelter, and recovery conditions for this analysis; they do not provide a single dollar figure for all of these coordination costs.

The people who bear the consequences are not just institutions or budgets. They are the person who cannot evacuate without assistance, the family without a safe place to sleep, the responder deciding which of many urgent reports to pursue, the worker whose livelihood disappears, and the neighborhood whose services and local spending do not return. The 2026 County and LAEDC economic update estimates that **about 38% of survivors had exhausted or would soon exhaust displacement assistance coverage**. It estimates more than **$150 million in annual local household-spending losses** across the Palisades and Eaton fire areas. The first economic update reported that the Eaton area accounted for **76% of affected businesses and 75% of job losses in the secondary fire areas**—evidence that disruption and its burdens extend beyond the burn perimeter and are unevenly distributed geographically; the report does not attribute those differences to resource allocation. ([County/LAEDC Economic Update #3](https://lacounty.gov/2026/06/08/la-county-deo-and-laedc-release-latest-economic-update-on-the-2025-wildfire-recovery/); [DEO/LAEDC initial economic analysis](https://opportunity.lacounty.gov/economictrendfire/))

## Fresh data and who gets to provide it

The shift is from the server-era premise that **“owning data is gold”** to the operational reality that **live data is valuable because it can still change what happens next**. Humanitarian information systems ask the immediate questions: where people are, what they urgently need, and who is best placed to help. United Nations material documents the growing use of real-time data for humanitarian and development work, while OCHA’s response guidance is built around information-management staff, agencies, and partners. ([OCHA guidance on emergency information management](https://knowledge.base.unocha.org/wiki/spaces/imtoolbox/pages/222200017/Types%2Bof%2BHumanitarian%2BResponses); [UN: Big Data for Sustainable Development](https://www.un.org/en/global-issues/big-data-for-sustainable-development))

That capability has largely been organized around institutions: agencies, NGOs, contractors, assessment teams, and the systems they operate. Has-Needs extends it to pedestrians. A bystander, resident, volunteer, or survivor can originate a location-relevant Need, Has, or observation; choose what to disclose through a persona; discover nearby people and offers; and receive a traceable response. The person contributes fresh information as a sovereign participant in a reciprocal exchange, not as raw material copied into someone else’s database.

This changes who can create and act on the value of live data. In the fire scenario, a person walking past a burning block may know which street is blocked; a resident may know who needs transport; a survivor under rubble may provide their location and condition. The information can begin where it exists and move through a consented, accountable path. The economic value is less time spent searching, repeating intake, and reconciling fragmented reports, alongside the ability to direct resources using current ground-level knowledge. A deployment can measure those efficiencies in dollars.

After disasters, people already organize spare rooms and other forms of help. Has-Needs is designed to make a specific offer easier to discover, give both sides control over disclosure and acceptance, and make the experience feel safer so more people will consider offering help. A person could request a safe place to sleep; a neighbor could offer a room for agreed dates and terms; both could accept, update, and close the exchange. Showing needs and capacities together gives people a fuller view of one another and makes empathy actionable. The design intent is to prolong the post-event benevolent phenomenon: to help spontaneous generosity remain visible and coordinated as the first wave of attention passes.

## Where emergency efficiency costs are saved

Emergency systems lose money when information arrives late, gets duplicated, or cannot reach the person able to act on it. Has-Needs addresses those failure points by letting people contribute local information directly, preserving each message’s source and status, and connecting Needs with available Hases through accepted commitments.

The savings can be counted across the response:

- **Communications and intake:** fewer repeated calls, duplicate forms, status checks, handoffs, and manual re-entry.
- **Dispatch and field operations:** fewer duplicate deployments, avoidable trips, and searches for information already reported nearby.
- **Resource allocation:** less capacity committed to the same visible Need while other Needs remain open; faster discovery of available local skills, supplies, transport, and shelter.
- **Network disruption:** less time rebuilding the picture after outages because nearby devices can share and relay authorized records as connections allow.
- **Evacuation and assistance:** less delay between a person expressing a Need, someone accepting it, and help arriving.
- **Shelter and recovery:** fewer unplaced nights and less time spent searching across disconnected offers, agencies, and households.
- **Administration and reimbursement:** less reconstruction of who requested, offered, accepted, delivered, and received each resource.

These costs arise because conventional systems concentrate intake and decision-making in channels that overload during emergencies. Calls queue, networks fail, agencies keep separate records, people repeat their stories, and available help stays invisible. Has-Needs distributes communication and matching across participating people and devices while keeping an accountable record of each exchange.

A broad comparison can make the scale understandable. If a flood response and recovery costs **$100 million** under fragmented coordination, and a Has-Needs deployment reduces communication and coordination costs by **$20 million**, the comparable total is **$80 million**. The reduction is justified by documented categories: labor hours avoided, duplicate deployments prevented, resource-hours no longer wasted, lodging nights covered sooner, and administrative effort removed. The precise result depends on the event and its records; the example shows how efficiency gains change the overall cost, not a guaranteed savings rate.

For a specific event, calculate each category from recorded hours, loaded labor rates, resource operating costs, duplicate activity, and duration of unmet Needs. Keep direct response costs, coordination costs, and human consequences visible together. That produces a broad event-level comparison while showing how the total was derived.

## The current Has-Needs workflow beside the records

Has-Needs starts with a sovereign person who can originate a **Need** or a **Has**. A Need states an outcome and its terms; a Has describes a capability, observation, resource, or other contribution. Local-first discovery lets participating devices find possible cooperation nearby and relay authorized information outward. A proposal becomes **WORKING** only when relevant participants accept it. The Need-owned pending journal preserves accepted commitments and authorized progress. At resolution, reciprocal receipts preserve the agreed outcome on the participants’ separate personal chains.

The current prototypes and components bring together the Persona Manager, eUTXO-based Need contracts, Has NFTs, DXOS local-first collaboration, OCA context, and Jitterbug discovery and routing. The architecture supports a different path through coordination: each person’s request and contribution keeps its origin and state, and each accepted exchange has a recorded outcome.

| Fire-record condition | Has-Needs representation | What the record can show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Many urgent reports arrive at once | Separate Needs and Hases retain their owners and states; related reports can be viewed together | Which Needs were new, repeated, acknowledged, accepted, unresolved, or resolved |
| A responder or resident has local information | A person originates a contextual Has or a Need through a chosen persona | Source, time, declared context, chosen disclosure, and authorized handoffs |
| Cellular service or central reporting is unavailable | Nearby participating devices discover and relay authorized messages locally; Jitterbug routes across peers as paths become available | Which peers received and relayed a message, when it reached a connected participant, and where it remained unresolved |
| Resources are committed to a request | Participants accept explicit terms into WORKING and record progress in the Need-owned journal | Resource type, commitment, accepted amount or duration, status, and outcome |
| A person needs shelter, transport, or another immediate contribution | A Need can match with a Has offered by a person, group, or institution | Time to match, acceptance, duration, fulfillment, and remaining need |
| An exchange is resolved | Participants preserve reciprocal canonical receipts on their separate chains | Who contributed, what was agreed, and what happened |

The record structure can reveal concentration and gaps; it does not decide whether an allocation is fair. That judgment needs an authorized policy and context. An evaluation should compare who received help, who waited, whose Needs remained unresolved, and how those results varied by geography, time, and access requirements. It should also show when one request attracted multiple overlapping offers while another received none.

## Why record value exchange

Has-Needs records value exchanges because people deserve recognition for what they agree to do for one another. A Need gives a person’s stated need standing; a Has makes another person’s capacity to help visible. Reciprocity creates an expectation of response. Recognition tells each person that their contribution matters. Agency, accountability, and being valued are central to trauma recovery; Has-Needs makes them part of how cooperation works. The reciprocal receipt proves each person’s part in the agreement and records the outcome as fact.

In an emergency, firsthand knowledge, a neighbor’s room or ride, and an organization’s defined commitment all become visible contributions. Secure provenance keeps each connected to its source through the decision chain. The record preserves contributions through message traffic and distinguishes them from repeats. Recognition, agency, and accountable cooperation emerge from the ordinary operation of the system.

## What this comparison establishes

The fire records show simultaneous demands, scarce resources, vulnerable communications, evacuation consequences, and a long recovery shaped by housing stability, access to services, volunteer coordination, and local economic disruption. The Has-Needs workflow gives these events a connected structure: a sovereign person, a Need or Has, discovery and relay, mutual acceptance, work, and reciprocal resolution.

That structure preserves each Need and contribution with its origin, status, commitments, and outcome. It places open requests beside available resources, makes overlaps and gaps visible, and keeps an accountable record of what people agreed to do and what happened. In disasters and everyday life, this supports agency, trust, empathy, and coordinated action.

The Los Angeles records give us a concrete setting to measure the gains: elapsed time, staff effort, resource use, unresolved Needs, and human outcomes. The comparison can show where coordination improves and how much value that improvement returns to communities.

Emergency coordination should preserve the person, request, offer, commitment, and outcome as one connected account of cooperation. Communities can see what is needed and available, allocate deliberately, and keep local generosity organized beyond the first response.

## Funding the system from a share of realized efficiency

The funding model follows the cost comparison above. A county pays no charge for each person, Need, Has, or exchange. Instead, Has-Needs receives a **one-time county-level share of 5% of verified realized efficiency**. The county retains the other **95%**.

The first deployment can be the funding event. A county can fund the deployment at the outset, then reconcile the amount due after a year, when response and recovery activity, costs, and outcomes have been tallied. At county scale, a single deployment could generate enough verified efficiency for the 5% share to cover the full system cost. The year-end close makes the amount auditable; it does not require guessing in advance.

The money trail is designed to support that calculation. Each Need has an owner, terms, status, and outcome. Each Has keeps its source and commitment. Accepted work, progress, and reciprocal receipts preserve what was promised and what happened. The county can connect those records to its own cost data: staff hours, resource-hours, duplicate intake, repeated verification, unused capacity, and unresolved Needs. This creates a gold-standard operational record for determining where time and money were spent and what changed.

| Verified realized efficiency | 5% county share | 95% retained by county and community |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1 million | $50,000 | $950,000 |
| $10 million | $500,000 | $9.5 million |
| $100 million | $5 million | $95 million |
| $1 billion | $50 million | $950 million |

These are illustrations of the rate, not estimates of LA fire savings. The LA record establishes the scale of emergency and recovery costs; a deployment would establish which coordination costs Has-Needs actually reduced. The share applies to verified efficiency, not to property destruction, insurance payments, total disaster spending, or human loss. The result is a direct incentive: the system is funded when the county can show that coordination worked better, while most of the realized value stays with the people and institutions it serves.
